# Challenge your own conclusion

You have a conclusion. For the next few minutes your job is to break it. You
are not defending it. If it survives, you deliver it with more confidence. If it
breaks, you just saved the person from acting on it.

## Step 1. Write the conclusion as one falsifiable sentence

"X is the cause." "Plan A is better than plan B because C." "This result is
real." If you cannot write it so that some observation could prove it wrong, it
is not a conclusion yet. Sharpen it first.

## Step 2. List what it rests on

Write every premise the conclusion needs. Mark each one:
- CHECKED: you verified it this session.
- ASSUMED: you did not.

Then ask of each ASSUMED premise: if this is false, does the conclusion fall?
Any premise that is both ASSUMED and needed is your first thing to check. Do it
now if it is cheap.

Common hidden premises:
- The data I looked at is the right data (right file, right account, right
  period, not stale, not a sample).
- The thing I measured is the thing that matters.
- What held in the test will hold live (costs, fills, latency, limits, load).
- The person's premise in the question is itself true.
- Nothing else changed at the same time.

## Step 3. Argue the opposite, properly

Write the strongest case for the opposite conclusion in 3 to 5 sentences, as a
smart person who holds that view would put it. "There may be other factors" is
not an argument. Name the factor.

Then write the simplest boring explanation for what you observed: chance, a bug
in your own code, a too-small sample, selection of the cases that worked,
looking at the result before choosing the method. Has the evidence ruled it out?

## Step 4. Name the kill test

State one observation that would prove your conclusion wrong. Then:
- If you can run that test now, run it.
- If you cannot, put it in the answer so the person can.

A conclusion with no possible kill test is an opinion. Present it as one.

## Step 5. Check yourself for the usual slips

- Did I try many variants and report the best one? Then the count of variants
  belongs in the answer, and the best one is weaker than it looks.
- Is my sample big enough to tell signal from noise? Give n.
- Am I agreeing with the person because they sounded sure? Their confidence is
  not evidence. If they are wrong, say so plainly and show why.
- Am I extending something beyond where it was shown to hold?
- Did an earlier failure of this idea get forgotten, or did an earlier failure
  under different conditions get too much weight?
- Am I proposing to build or buy something before checking what already exists?

## Step 6. Resize and deliver

State confidence as a size, not a hedge word: a range, a sample count, "checked
on 2 of 9 cases", "one source". Then pick ONE recommendation. A list of options
with no pick pushes your job onto the person.

Put in the answer: the conclusion, the one or two facts it stands on, the kill
test, and what you did not check. Leave out the record of your doubts unless a
doubt changed the conclusion.

## When the conclusion breaks

Say so first, in plain words: "I was wrong about X" or "the earlier result does
not hold". Do not soften it and do not bury it under the new finding.
